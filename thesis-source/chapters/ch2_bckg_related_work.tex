\chapter{Background and Related Work}

This chapter reviews the theoretical foundations and related work necessary for building a scene-aware dialogue system. It progresses from the high-level requirements of human-robot interaction down to the specific visual perception models, structured scene representations, and efficient multimodal language generation techniques utilized in this thesis.

\section{Social Robotics and Grounded Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)}

Social robots operate in shared human environments where users expect them to exhibit awareness of their physical and social surroundings. Recent integrations of Large Language Models (LLMs) into human-robot interaction (HRI) have significantly improved conversational fluency. However, as Kim et al.~\cite{kim_understanding_2024} and Atuhurra et al.~\cite{atuhurra_leveraging_2024} note, this fluency often raises user expectations: when a robot speaks naturally, users implicitly assume it also understands the physical context and social nuances of the conversation.

To prevent a mismatch between linguistic competence and actual situational awareness, the robot's dialogue must be grounded. In robotics, grounding denotes the explicit connection between linguistic expressions (e.g., ``the person on my left'') and the physical entities to which they refer. Unlike a purely text-based assistant, an embodied robot must continuously resolve these references against its changing perceptual state.

The Pepper robot is a prominent platform for HRI research due to its humanoid form, native tablet interface~\cite{softbank_peppers_tablet_2026}, and built-in speech recognition~\cite{softbank_alspeechrecognition_2026}. However, its onboard computational hardware is severely limited and insufficient for modern deep learning inference. Consequently, achieving near real-time scene understanding on Pepper requires a distributed architecture, where heavy visual and linguistic processing is offloaded to an external server while the robot handles interaction and sensory acquisition~\cite{reyes_near_2018, trinquet_future_2025, brad_retrieval-augmented_2025}.

Recent literature demonstrates the viability of augmenting Pepper with large language models and modern vision across diverse applications. For instance, Brad et al.~\cite{brad_retrieval-augmented_2025} implemented RAG architectures for Pepper in industrial assistance, Latif et al.~\cite{latif_physicsassistant_2024} deployed Pepper as a YOLO-equipped ``PhysicsAssistant'' in educational labs, and Zabala et al.~\cite{zabala_exploring_2025} explored multimodal collaborative storytelling where Pepper recognizes physical objects to co-create narratives. Furthermore, Wang et al.~\cite{wang_target_2024} and Becerra et al.~\cite{becerra_improving_2024} have successfully integrated YOLO and LLMs to improve Pepper's target grasping and autonomous instruction execution.

Beyond conversational fluency, LLMs are increasingly utilized as high-level semantic planners for embodied agents. The SayCan framework~\cite{ahn_as_2022} pioneered this by grounding LLM reasoning in physical robotic affordances, ensuring that proposed actions are actually executable in the real world. Similarly, paradigms like ``Code as Policies''~\cite{liang_code_2023} demonstrate that LLMs can directly synthesize Python code to map visual perception to robot control primitives. In the context of humanoid robots, Catalini et al.~\cite{catalini_llms_nodate} have demonstrated the capability of LLMs to act as 3D motion planners, generating sequences of joint angles for the NAO robot directly from natural language prompts.


\section{Visual Perception for Robotics}

The foundation of scene awareness is object detection. Traditionally, robotics has relied on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), such as the YOLO family, which utilize anchor boxes and dense grid predictions. While fast, these models require heuristic post-processing like Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). Recently, the architectural shift toward Detection Transformers (DETRs)~\cite{zhao_detrs_2024} has reframed detection as a direct set-prediction problem. By leveraging self-attention mechanisms~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed} and bipartite matching during training, models like RT-DETR and RF-DETR eliminate NMS, providing state-of-the-art accuracy while maintaining the real-time latency required for interactive robotics.

Standard detectors operate on a closed vocabulary, mapping visual features to a fixed set of classes via a classifier head. While computationally efficient, these models are heavily constrained by their training datasets, such as Microsoft COCO~\cite{lin2015microsoftcococommonobjects}. A model trained on COCO is highly proficient at identifying generic classes (e.g., ``giraffe'', ``zebra'', ``stop sign''), but lacks the specialized vocabulary required for an office or computer lab environment. Re-training these models for new environments requires costly dataset annotation. Open-vocabulary models, such as Grounding DINO~\cite{liu_grounding_2024} and OWL-ViT~\cite{minderer_simple_2022}, bypass this limitation by marrying transformer architectures with grounded language pre-training. By projecting image patches and text prompts into a shared multimodal embedding space (often aligned via CLIP~\cite{pmlr-v139-radford21a}), these models allow the robot to detect arbitrary, environment-specific objects (e.g., ``a blue ethernet cable'') purely from textual prompts, without any fine-tuning.

The shift toward multimodal architectures has been heavily driven by the introduction of the Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}. Unlike CNNs, which process images through hierarchical convolutional filters, ViTs divide an image into a grid of fixed-size patches. These patches are linearly embedded and treated as a sequence of tokens, analogous to text processing. This shared token-based architecture is the theoretical foundation that allows modern open-vocabulary models to seamlessly align visual and textual features. Furthermore, this thesis explores specific implementations of these paradigms, utilizing YOLO for high-speed closed-set baselines~\cite{zhao2024detrsbeatyolosrealtime}, RF-DETR~\cite{robinson2026rfdetrneuralarchitecturesearch} for state-of-the-art transformer-based detection, and scaling laws in open-vocabulary detection as demonstrated by recent iterations of the OWL architecture~\cite{minderer2024scalingopenvocabularyobjectdetection}. As Feng et al.~\cite{feng_vision-language_2025} note, evaluating these Vision-Language Models for raw detection reveals trade-offs between zero-shot flexibility and closed-set precision. Discussing the pros and cons of these methods forms a critical part of the system design, as the trade-off between inference speed, vocabulary flexibility, and architectural complexity directly dictates the robot's real-time conversational capabilities.

Detecting an object in a single frame is insufficient for conversation; the system must preserve object permanence. In standard high-framerate video pipelines, Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) algorithms like Deep SORT~\cite{wojke_simple_2017} and ByteTrack~\cite{zhang_bytetrack_2022} achieve state-of-the-art identity association by heavily relying on the spatial continuity of bounding boxes between consecutive frames (measured via Intersection over Union, IoU). However, this spatial continuity presupposition is completely violated in the embodied architecture proposed in this thesis. Because Pepper surveys its environment by executing discrete head movements (e.g., panning left, center, right) and acquiring separate images at different angles, objects exhibit massive spatial displacement between observations. Consequently, classical tracking algorithms fail. To solve this, the system eschews classical frame-to-frame tracking in favor of repeated detection combined with appearance-based re-identification (Re-ID). By utilizing powerful visual embedding models, such as C-RADIOv4~\cite{ranzinger2026cradiov4techreport}, the system extracts semantic appearance vectors for each detected crop. Object permanence is thus maintained by comparing these embeddings across discontinuous viewpoints, augmented by high-level scene geometry, ensuring robust identity association even across disjointed observations.
\section{Semantic Scene Understanding and Scene Graphs}

While bounding boxes localize objects, they lack the relational semantics required for language generation. Scene Graphs~\cite{johnson_image_2015} bridge this gap by providing a structured intermediate representation---a directed graph where nodes represent detected objects and edges represent semantic or spatial relationships (e.g., \verb|<cup> [on] <table_>|).

In addition to pairwise spatial relations, objects possess unary properties such as color or state. In this work, to maintain a uniform graph structure, attributes are elegantly modeled as self-referential edges (unary relations) on the object nodes. This unifies the representation, allowing the dialogue system to query properties and spatial relations using identical graph traversal logic.

Scene Graph Generation (SGG) can be achieved through heuristic geometric rules or learned models. Learned approaches, such as RelTR (Relation Transformer)~\cite{cong_reltr_2023}, treat SGG as a direct set-prediction problem, inferring subject-predicate-object triplets from image features. Reflecting the consensus that hierarchical, persistent representations are central to robust robot understanding~\cite{mascaro_scene_2025, hughes_foundations_2024}, this thesis employs a hybrid neuro-symbolic approach, combining fast geometric rules for stable spatial relations with learned multimodal prompting for richer semantic connections.

As highlighted in the comprehensive survey by Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu_scene_2022}, SGG has evolved from simple bounding-box pairing into a fundamental paradigm for high-level scene understanding. Recent advancements demonstrate that Vision-Language Models can perform zero-shot SGG in highly complex domains, such as the IndVisSGG framework for industrial spatial intelligence~\cite{wang_indvissgg_2025}. 

However, extracting Scene Graphs from generative VLMs typically requires forcing the model to output strict structural formats, such as JSON or XML. This introduces a significant architectural trade-off. Recent research by Tam et al.~\cite{tam_let_2024} reveals that imposing strict format restrictions on large language models substantially degrades their underlying reasoning abilities. The cognitive overhead required to maintain rigid syntactic grammar detracts from the model's capacity for spatial reasoning and semantic comprehension. This highlights the necessity of the hybrid neuro-symbolic approach proposed in this thesis: offloading rigid geometric spatial relations to deterministic rules allows the VLM to focus its reasoning capacity purely on complex semantic interactions, rather than struggling to format basic spatial data.


In academic literature, the performance of Scene Graph Generation is typically evaluated across three standard sub-tasks, reflecting varying degrees of reliance on the underlying object detector. Predicate Classification (PredCls) isolates relation accuracy by providing the model with ground-truth object labels and bounding boxes. Scene Graph Classification (SGCls) provides ground-truth bounding boxes but requires the model to predict both object labels and relations. Finally, Scene Graph Detection (SGDet) is the strict end-to-end task, requiring the system to independently localize objects, classify them, and predict their relationships. Establishing these distinct levels of evaluation is critical for isolating the performance of the relational reasoning layer from the raw localization layer.


\section{Multimodal AI: LLMs, VLMs, and Efficient Architectures}

To generate grounded dialogue, the structured scene representation must be connected to natural language via Large Language Models (LLMs). LLMs are built upon the Transformer architecture, utilizing self-attention mechanisms for autoregressive next-token prediction~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}.

However, a base autoregressive model is optimized merely to predict the statistical continuation of a text corpus, which does not inherently make it a useful conversational agent. To function in a dialogue system, these base models undergo Instruction Tuning and alignment processes---often utilizing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)~\cite{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow}. This alignment transforms the model from a simple text-completer into an instruction-following agent capable of answering questions, declining unsafe requests, and maintaining a coherent conversational persona.
 

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) extend this capability by conditioning text generation on visual inputs~\cite{liu_visual_2023, dai_instructblip_2023, driess_palm-e_2023}. Typically, a Vision Encoder (such as a ViT) extracts features from an image, which are then projected into the LLM's embedding space. To the LLM, the image functions as a sequence of ``visual words'' appended to the text prompt.

As models scale to achieve better reasoning, computational costs rise dramatically. A dense model activates all its parameters for every token. To mitigate this, modern architectures frequently employ a Mixture of Experts (MoE) routing mechanism~\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks}. In an MoE model, only a small subset of specialized neural ``experts'' is activated for any given token. Consequently, if a system possesses sufficient RAM or VRAM to load the model weights, it can achieve the reasoning capabilities of a massive parameter model at the inference speed and compute cost of a much smaller model. This architectural efficiency is highly advantageous in robotics, where rapid inference is critical.


The frontier of multimodal AI is moving toward models that natively integrate spatial localization into their text generation. Kosmos-2~\cite{peng_kosmos-2_2023}, for example, represents spatial referring expressions as explicit Markdown links tied to bounding box coordinates, establishing a direct convergence between language generation and world modeling.

Despite their capabilities, VLMs are prone to hallucinating relations or failing to disambiguate multiple instances of the same object. To enforce precise visual grounding, this thesis utilizes Set-of-Mark (SoM) prompting~\cite{yang_set-of-mark_2023}. By rendering explicit visual markers---such as numbered bounding boxes or segmentation masks~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023}---directly onto the image, SoM transforms visual regions into explicit, referable symbols. This forces the VLM to anchor its relational reasoning to specific, tracked detections.

\section{Grounded Dialogue and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)}

Classical robotic dialogue systems historically rely on rigid, intent-based dialogue management architectures, such as Rasa~\cite{bocklisch_rasa_2017} or Pepper's native QiChat engine. While highly deterministic, these systems require developers to manually script every possible conversational branch, rendering them entirely incapable of handling open-ended questions about an unpredictable visual scene. Generative AI offers open-ended flexibility, provided the generation can be tethered to the robot's physical reality. In this thesis, the deterministic reliability of classical rule-based triggers (e.g., QiChat) is merged with the generative capacity of LLMs: the legacy engine routes user intent, while the LLM dynamically generates the grounded response.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis_retrieval-augmented_2020} provides a framework for enriching language models with external data. In modern AI architectures, executing RAG over multimodal data typically involves Multi-Vector Retrievers~\cite{noauthor_multi-vector_nodate}. Historically, this paradigm required passing visual modalities (images, tables) through a VLM to generate textual summaries, which were then embedded and stored in a vector database for standard semantic retrieval. While recent advancements by Google have introduced fully functional multimodal embedding spaces capable of natively indexing images and text concurrently~\cite{noauthor_multi-vector_nodate}, executing dense vector search remains computationally heavy. As Fazlollahtabar~\cite{fazlollahtabar_human-robot_2025} notes, integrating RAG with transformer networks is becoming a cornerstone of advanced Human-Robot Interaction.

However, the approach adopted in this thesis diverges from both probabilistic dense vector search and textual summary retrieval. By continuously updating a deterministic, symbolic representation---the Scene Graph---the system performs real-time structured extraction of the scene state. Because the scene representation is highly compact, it fits entirely within the LLM's context window. This transitions the pipeline from Retrieval-Augmented Generation to Cache-Augmented Generation (CAG)~\cite{Chan_2025}, eliminating the latency of vector search while guaranteeing absolute factual alignment with the robot's current perception.

Crucially, this structured approach shifts the cognitive burden placed on the LLM. Rather than relying on its internal Parametric Memory to hallucinate an answer, the model is tasked with ``reading comprehension'' over the injected context. By providing the exact physical state in the prompt, even relatively small LLMs can perform exceptionally well on knowledge tasks. Coupled with the MoE architectures discussed previously, this strategy drastically reduces VRAM requirements and inference latency, enabling highly responsive, factually grounded HRI on computationally constrained platforms.
