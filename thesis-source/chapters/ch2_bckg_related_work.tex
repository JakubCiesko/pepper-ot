%page 230: 4.5 Task-Shaping Robotic technology is introduced to a domain either to allow a human to do a task that they could not do before, or to make the task easier or more pleasant for the human. Implicit in this assertion is the fact that introducing technology fundamentally changes the way that humans do the task. Task-shaping is a term that emphasizes the importance of considering how the task should be done and will be done when new technology is introduced. Compared to the other ways that a designer can shape HRI, there is little written about task-shaping. There are formal processes for understanding how the task should be done and is currently done. These processes include goal-directed task analyses, cognitive work analyses, and ethnographic studies [58, 88, 301]. Although frequently used to specify how a task is done and how it should be done, it is imperative to consider how the task will be done, including unintended consequences of design [14, 212]. One reason that little is written about task-shaping is because designers are implicitly trying to create technology and interactions that accomplish some task or function. Indeed, Woods has persuasively argued that designing a system is equivalent to making a hypothesis about how the artifact will positively shape the experience [319]. Nevertheless, it is important to consider how the task might be modified to better support interaction. Examples of explicit task-shaping include designing space or underwater equipment and tools so that handles and connectors can be manipulated by a robotic arm, “pre-cleaning” a room so that a robot vacuum can accomplish its task most efficiently [99], and performing pre-inspection tasks used to form maps and plans that can be executed by a robot

Furthermore, consensus is lacking in terms of understanding what these robots do and what, specifically, makes them social. Within the field of HRI, social robots take on a special role, and fall under the category of ‘proximate interaction’, in which ‘humans and robots interact as peers or companions’ [18]. Based on reference information of articles they extracted, Mejia and Kajikawa [19] identified relevant clusters that represent the social robotics knowledgebase. The largest clusters in social robotics research can be summarized as ‘robots as social partners’ and ‘human factors and ergonomics in human-robot interaction’. Interestingly, the authors point out that research trends emphasize the various fields of application for social robots: robots as companions, robots as educators for children, and robots as assistants for the elderly. This is consistent with a trend identified by Šabanović, who, in interviews with robotics researchers in the USA and Japan, identified that social robots ‘often represent technological fixes’ i.e. using a technological approach to solve a pressing societal problem ([20], p. 349). Sarrica and colleagues [21] investigated the question of how social robots are understood by analyzing definitions in articles published by the International Journal of Social Robotics between 2009 and 2015. In investigating the most often cited definitions, it becomes apparent how heterogenous the understanding of social robots is. Through this work, Sarrica and colleagues identified a few shared traits: social robots are physically embodied agents that have some (or full) autonomy and engage in social interactions with humans, by communicating, cooperating, and making decisions. These behaviours are then interpreted by human onlookers as ‘social’, according to current norms and conventions. A study by de Graaf, Allouch, and van Dijk [6] evaluated users’ perspectives on the characteristics of social HRI through a longitudinal home study. They observed and identified eight main social characteristics that users described as factors for a social robot to appear as social and be accepted as social entities in their homes. The most prominent factor was (1) the capability of two-way interaction, expecting a robot to be able to respond to a human in a social manner. When a robot failed to do so, people were disappointed and experienced a sense of dissonance. Following this, users described the need for robots to share the same environment as them (be physically embodied or embedded), and to: (2) display thoughts and feelings; (3) be socially aware of their environment; (4) provide social support by being there for them (like their friends); and (5) demonstrate autonomy. Participants also raised the concepts of (6) cosiness, (7) similarity to self; and (8) mutual respect. However, these latter three concepts were mentioned fewer times than the previous five concepts. While users’ perceptions of robots’ socialness share many similarities with scholars’ definitions of social robots, some key differences also emerge. Users’ expectations, as described in de Graaf and colleagues’ [6] study, were influenced by their relationships with other social actors (i.e. their friends). Participants repeatedly compared the robot in that study to their friends, dwelling on the fact that the robot’s lack of social capabilities meant that it would be unlikely to become an actual ‘friend’. By contrast, the definitions of a social robot described in Sarrica and colleagues’ [21] review focus on general social and communication capabilities. It is of note, however, that these definitions rarely address the context of the interaction, whose importance is underscored by the findings of de Graaf and colleagues [6]. This discrepancy has been noted in other user studies as well. Dautenhahn and colleagues [22] show that participants in their studies did not see robots as companions or friends, but rather as useful household servants. Dereshev and colleagues [7] interviewed long-term, expert users of the Pepper robot (SoftBank Robotics; seen in Fig. 1). Their participants had lived and interacted with the robot on timescales ranging between 8 months to more than 3 years. The researchers report that one specific expectation regarding the humanoid Pepper robot was its ability to engage in a reciprocal conversation. Participants were disappointed when the robot was not able to go beyond the smart-speaker like single-turn structure of conversation. One of the participants also pointed out that people who interacted with Pepper quickly lost interest, a finding which is echoed in a usability study by Aldebaran (later purchased by SoftBank Robotics), where Pepper was deployed to the homes of users over several weeks [8]. The novelty effect is a common problem in social robotics, and long-term studies have often found a reduced engagement with various robotic platforms over time [23, 24]. Finally, Baraka and colleagues [25] recently proposed an ‘extended framework’ for social robotics by illustrating seven relevant dimensions of social robots: a robot’s (1) appearance, (2) social capabilities, (3) autonomy, (4) intelligence, the (5) proximity and (6) temporal profile of the interaction, and the (7) context of the interaction (such as its purpose or intended application). In their appearance classification system, they distinguish between bio-inspired robots (e.g. human- or animal-inspired), artefact shaped (e.g. robots resembling man-made objects or those that are imaginary), and functional robots (e.g. drones). Additional recent efforts to establish frameworks for designing and evaluating social robotics research emphasize that in all the enthusiasm from researchers from different fields to amplify or focus on social aspects of social robots, these robots remain, at their core, machines, and advances in HRI research will be well served to keep robots’ machine or object-like qualities in mind as well [26].

Human-robot interaction (HRI) studies interaction between humans and robots. Goodrich and Schultz define HRI as the field concerned with understanding and shaping interaction between one or more humans and one or more robots, and distinguish between remote interaction and proximate interaction \cite[204, 216]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}. 

In remote interaction, the human and robot are separated spatially or temporally, as in search-and-rescue or space robotics. In proximate interaction, humans and robots share the same physical environment making safety, communication, as well as timing, and social interpretation central design concerns \cite[213--215]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}.

HRI is not limited to humanoid or human-like looking robots. Goodrich and Schultz discuss domains such as search-and-rescue, assistive robotics, and space exploration, where the robot's form is determined primarily by the task rather than by social appearance \cite[213--215]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}. 

A rescue robot, for example, may need to enter rubble or carry sensors. In such cases, human-like embodiment would often be unnecessary or counterproductive. What makes these systems relevant to HRI is not their appearance, but the need to coordinate human and robotic capabilities through effective interaction.

Goodrich and Schultz further identify several dimensions through which designers shape HRI: the level and behaviour of autonomy, the nature of information exchange, the structure of the human-robot team, adaptation and learning, and the shape of the task itself \cite[216--230]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}. 

For this thesis, information exchange is particularly important. Interaction quality depends on how intent, instructions, feedback, and situational information are communicated between the human and the robot. This communication may involve visual displays, gestures, speech and natural language, non-speech audio, physical interaction, or haptics \cite[221]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}. %Scene-aware dialogue can therefore be understood as one specific form of information exchange: the robot uses language to communicate about the shared physical situation.

Social robotics forms a more specific part of HRI and is usually associated with proximate interaction. Henschel et al. note that no universally accepted definition of social robots exists, but identify recurring features: social robots are physically embodied agents with some degree of autonomy that engage humans through communication, cooperation, and socially interpretable behaviour \cite[11--12]{henschel_what_2021}. Social robots are commonly studied as companions, educators, assistants, or care-related technologies. In these roles, the robot is an interactive agent sharing space with the user.

One interesting aspect of proximity is a change in user expectations. Henschel et al. report that users often expect social robots to support two-way interaction, share the same environment, show social awareness, and sustain interaction beyond short novelty effects \cite[11--12]{henschel_what_2021}. 


Studies of Pepper users similarly indicate that people may expect reciprocal conversation and lose interest when the interaction remains limited to single-turn, smart-speaker-like exchanges \cite[12]{henschel_what_2021}. This is relevant for Pepper because its humanoid form and expressive behaviour invite expectations of social and contextual competence.

Embodiment can therefore support interaction, but it can also create a mismatch between appearance and capability. Adams et al. argue that social robots need to detect and use natural human cues such as nods, gaze, and eye contact so that people can interact with them without special training \cite[25]{adams_humanoid_2000}. 

More recent work similarly reports that users may expect physical expressiveness or actions that the robot cannot perform, creating functional mismatches between embodied appearance and actual capability \cite{app15116377}. A small pilot study comparing a humanoid robot with a smartphone application also suggests that embodiment may improve perceived reliability, naturalness, and willingness to interact again, although these findings should be treated cautiously because of the limited sample size.\footnote{The study by Gava et al. involved only 12 participants, so its findings are suggestive rather than conclusive.} \cite{gava2024physical}

Recent integrations of large language models (LLMs) into HRI intensify this issue. LLMs can make robots more fluent, flexible, and conversationally engaging, but fluency may also increase the expectation that the robot understands the physical and social context of the interaction \cite{kim_understanding_2024,atuhurra_leveraging_2024}. When a robot speaks naturally, users may assume that it can also perceive nearby objects or interpret references to the shared environment.

For this reason, successful social interaction depends on more than language generation. In proximate HRI, useful communication often concerns nearby objects, spatial relations, ongoing events, and references established earlier in the conversation. A socially capable robot should therefore connect dialogue to the surrounding situation and maintain coherence over time. This requirement motivates the scene-aware dialogue problem addressed in this thesis.

Selective search makes the process more efficient by proposing an initial set of bounding boxes that are good candidates to contain an object (Figure 50.13). This proposal mechanism is performed by a selection mechanism simpler than the object classifier. This approach was motivated by the strategy used by the visual system in which attention is first directed toward image regions likely to contain the target [11], [12], [13]. This first attentional mechanism is very fast but might be wrong. Therefore, a second, more accurate but also more expensive, processing stage is required in order to take a reliable decision. The advantage of this cascade of decisions is that the most expensive classifier is only applied to a sparse set of locations (the ones selected by the cheap attentional mechanism) dramatically reducing the overall computational cost. The algorithm for selective search only differs from the window scanning approach on how the list of candidate bounding boxes is generated. Some approaches also add a bounding box refinement step. The overall approach is described in algorithm Figure 50.12. Figure 50.12: Selective search algorithm. This algorithm has four main steps. In the first step, the algorithm uses an efficient window scanning approach to produce a set of candidate bounding boxes. In the second step, we loop over all the candidate bounding boxes, we crop out the box and resize it to a canonical size, and then we apply an object classifier to classify the cropped image as containing the object we are looking for or not. In the third step, we refine the bounding box for the crops that are classified as containing the object. And finally, in the fourth step, we remove low-scoring boxes and we use nonmaximum suppression (NMS) to discard overlapping detections likely to correspond to the same object, so as to output only one bounding box for each instance present in the image. It is an iterative algorithm as follows: (1) Take the highest confidence bounding box from the set and add it to the final set . For instance, digital cameras detect faces and use bounding boxes to encode their location. The camera uses the pixels inside the bounding box to automatically set the focus and exposure time. But using bounding boxes to represent the location objects will not be appropriate for objects with long and thin structures or for regions that do not have well-defined boundaries (Figure 50.20). It is useful to differentiate between stuff and objects. Stuff refers to things that do not have well-defined boundaries such as grass, water, and sky (we already discussed this in chapter Chapter 28). But the distinction between stuff and objects is not very sharp. In some images, object instances might be easy to separate like the two trees in the left image below, or become a texture where instances cannot be detected individually (as shown in the right image). In cases with lots of instances, bounding boxes might not appropriate and it is better to represent the region as “trees” than to try to detect each instance. Figure 50.20: Bounding boxes are not an appropriate representation of the location of trees. This is true for many object classes with extended shapes that are hard to approximate with a box. Even when there are few instances, bounding boxes can provide an ambiguous localization when two objects overlap because it might not be clear which pixels belong to each object. Bounding boxes are also an insufficient object description if the task is robot manipulation. A robot will need a more detailed description of the pose and shape of an object to interact with it. Let’s face it, localizing objects with bounding boxes does not address most of the shortcomings present in the image classification formulation. In fact, it adds a few more.

% https://www.ultralytics.com/glossary/object-re-identification-re-id: Object Re-identification (Re-ID) is a specialized task in computer vision (CV) designed to match a specific object or individual across different non-overlapping camera views or over extended periods. While standard object detection focuses on recognizing the class of an entity—identifying that an image contains a "person" or a "car"—Re-ID goes a step further by determining which specific person or car it is based on visual appearance. This capability is essential for creating a cohesive narrative of movement in large-scale environments where a single camera cannot cover the entire area, effectively connecting the dots between isolated visual observations. How Re-Identification Works The core challenge of Re-ID is maintaining identity consistency despite variations in lighting, camera angles, pose, and background clutter. To achieve this, the system typically employs a multi-step pipeline involving deep neural networks. Feature Extraction: Once an object is detected, a convolutional neural network (CNN) analyzes the image crop to generate a feature vector, commonly known as an embedding. This vector is a dense numerical representation of the object's unique visual traits, such as clothing texture or vehicle color. Metric Learning: The underlying models are trained using metric learning techniques. The goal is to ensure that embeddings of the same object are mathematically close together in vector space, while embeddings of different objects are pushed far apart. Specialized architectures like Siamese neural networks are often used to learn these relationships. Similarity Matching: During deployment, the system compares the embedding of a query object against a gallery of stored identities. This comparison usually involves calculating the cosine similarity or Euclidean distance. If the similarity score crosses a predefined threshold, the system confirms a match. Re-ID vs. Object Tracking It is important to distinguish Re-ID from object tracking, as they serve complementary but distinct roles in a vision pipeline. Object Tracking: This process relies on temporal continuity. Algorithms like the Kalman Filter predict an object's future location in the very next frame based on its current velocity and trajectory. It often uses Intersection over Union (IoU) to associate detections in adjacent frames. Re-Identification: Re-ID is crucial when temporal continuity is broken. This occurs during occlusion—when an object is hidden behind an obstacle—or when an object leaves one camera's field of view and enters another. Re-ID re-establishes identity based on appearance rather than location history, enabling robust multi-object tracking (MOT). Real-World Applications The ability to maintain identity across disjointed views allows for sophisticated analytics in various industries. Smart City Traffic Management: In the context of AI in smart cities, Re-ID allows municipal systems to track a vehicle as it moves through a city-wide network of intersections. This assists in calculating average travel times and optimizing traffic light timing without relying solely on license plate recognition. Retail Customer Analytics: Retailers utilize Re-ID to understand shopper behavior. By linking sightings of a customer across different aisles, stores can generate heatmaps of popular paths. This helps in optimizing store layouts and staffing levels, providing insights into the entire customer journey rather than just isolated interactions.

The foundation of scene awareness is object detection. Traditionally, robotics has relied on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), such as the YOLO family, which utilize anchor boxes and dense grid predictions. While fast, these models require heuristic post-processing like Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). Recently, the architectural shift toward Detection Transformers (DETRs)~\cite{zhao_detrs_2024} has reframed detection as a direct set-prediction problem. By leveraging self-attention mechanisms~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed} and bipartite matching during training, models like RT-DETR and RF-DETR eliminate NMS, providing state-of-the-art accuracy while maintaining the real-time latency required for interactive robotics.

Standard detectors operate on a closed vocabulary, mapping visual features to a fixed set of classes via a classifier head. While computationally efficient, these models are heavily constrained by their training datasets, such as Microsoft COCO~\cite{lin2015microsoftcococommonobjects}. A model trained on COCO is highly proficient at identifying generic classes (e.g., ``giraffe'', ``zebra'', ``stop sign''), but lacks the specialized vocabulary required for an office or computer lab environment. Re-training these models for new environments requires costly dataset annotation. Open-vocabulary models, such as Grounding DINO~\cite{liu_grounding_2024} and OWL-ViT~\cite{minderer_simple_2022}, bypass this limitation by marrying transformer architectures with grounded language pre-training. By projecting image patches and text prompts into a shared multimodal embedding space (often aligned via CLIP~\cite{pmlr-v139-radford21a}), these models allow the robot to detect arbitrary, environment-specific objects (e.g., ``a blue ethernet cable'') purely from textual prompts, without any fine-tuning.

The shift toward multimodal architectures has been heavily driven by the introduction of the Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}. Unlike CNNs, which process images through hierarchical convolutional filters, ViTs divide an image into a grid of fixed-size patches. These patches are linearly embedded and treated as a sequence of tokens, analogous to text processing. This shared token-based architecture is the theoretical foundation that allows modern open-vocabulary models to seamlessly align visual and textual features. Furthermore, this thesis explores specific implementations of these paradigms, utilizing YOLO for high-speed closed-set baselines~\cite{zhao2024detrsbeatyolosrealtime}, RF-DETR~\cite{robinson2026rfdetrneuralarchitecturesearch} for state-of-the-art transformer-based detection, and scaling laws in open-vocabulary detection as demonstrated by recent iterations of the OWL architecture~\cite{minderer2024scalingopenvocabularyobjectdetection}. As Feng et al.~\cite{feng_vision-language_2025} note, evaluating these Vision-Language Models for raw detection reveals trade-offs between zero-shot flexibility and closed-set precision. Discussing the pros and cons of these methods forms a critical part of the system design, as the trade-off between inference speed, vocabulary flexibility, and architectural complexity directly dictates the robot's real-time conversational capabilities.

Detecting an object in a single frame is insufficient for conversation; the system must preserve object permanence. In standard high-framerate video pipelines, Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) algorithms like Deep SORT~\cite{wojke_simple_2017} and ByteTrack~\cite{zhang_bytetrack_2022} achieve state-of-the-art identity association by heavily relying on the spatial continuity of bounding boxes between consecutive frames (measured via Intersection over Union, IoU). Because Pepper surveys its environment by executing discrete head movements (e.g., panning left, center, right) and acquiring separate images at different angles, objects exhibit massive spatial displacement between observations. Consequently, classical tracking algorithms fail. To solve this, the system eschews classical frame-to-frame tracking in favor of repeated detection combined with appearance-based re-identification (Re-ID). By utilizing powerful visual embedding models, such as C-RADIOv4~\cite{ranzinger2026cradiov4techreport}, the system extracts semantic appearance vectors for each detected crop. Object permanence is thus maintained by comparing these embeddings across discontinuous viewpoints, augmented by high-level scene geometry, ensuring robust identity association even across disjointed observations.

under real-time constraints so that detector variants can be selected for different compute budgets. In this thesis, this is relevant because interactive robotics imposes strict latency ceilings; the

A scene graph represents a scene as objects, attributes, and relations \cite{johnson_image_2015}. Let
\[
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}),
\]
where \(\mathcal{V}\) is a set of object instances and \(\mathcal{E}\) is a set of labeled edges. Each edge is a triplet \((s,p,o)\), where \(s\) is subject, \(p\) is predicate, and \(o\) is object. In
this thesis, unary attributes are encoded in the same triplet format as self-relations (\(s=o\)), which keeps one uniform data structure for querying both attributes and pairwise relations.

Scene graph generation (SGG) is the task of mapping visual input to this structured graph \cite{zhu_scene_2022}. The literature includes both geometric/rule-based methods and learned methods. Rule-
based approaches are deterministic and interpretable but limited in semantic richness. Learned approaches are more expressive but can be sensitive to domain shift, class imbalance, and output-format
constraints \cite{zhu_scene_2022}.

For learned SGG, this thesis uses RelTR as one backend. RelTR formulates SGG as end-to-end set prediction with a transformer encoder-decoder architecture \cite{cong_reltr_2023}. The encoder models
global visual context, and the decoder predicts a fixed-size set of \((\text{sub},\text{pred},\text{obj})\) triplets using coupled subject-object queries. Training uses matching-based set loss, which
avoids exhaustive pairwise relation enumeration and supports sparse one-stage prediction \cite{cong_reltr_2023}.

This thesis also uses a VLM-based SGG backend. VLM-based methods generate relation triplets directly from image evidence and prompts, including domain-specific ontologies that can be difficult to cover
with fixed supervised label spaces. Recent work such as IndVisSGG shows that VLM-based pipelines can produce useful scene graphs with good transfer to new industrial settings and without additional
object-level annotation in their method setup \cite{wang_indvissgg_2025}. This is relevant for robotics deployments where scene semantics vary across environments.

A practical issue is output formatting. Many SGG pipelines require strict machine-readable outputs (e.g., JSON triplets). However, stricter format constraints can reduce reasoning quality in LLM-style
generation settings \cite{tam_let_2024}. For this reason, the architecture in this thesis is hybrid: deterministic geometric rules handle stable spatial predicates, while learned backends (RelTR and
VLM prompting) contribute semantic and interaction relations. The final graph is obtained by merging and filtering backend outputs under one controlled ontology.

Grounding quality also depends on unambiguous visual references. To improve referential precision, the system uses Set-of-Mark style object-indexed prompting, where explicit object markers are rendered
in the visual input before VLM relation extraction \cite{yang_set-of-mark_2023}. This reduces ambiguity when multiple similar instances are present and supports consistent linking between detection
IDs, graph nodes, and dialogue references.

In the SGG literature, evaluation is commonly reported under protocols such as Predicate Classification (PredCls), Scene Graph Classification (SGCls), and Scene Graph Detection (SGDet), which separate
relation prediction difficulty from detector dependence to different extents \cite{zhu_scene_2022}. Because this thesis builds graphs from detected instances in an end-to-end embodied pipeline, its
practical setting is closest to SGDet conditions, where detection quality and relation quality jointly affect downstream dialogue behavior.


\section{Language modeling:\@ LLMs, VLMs}

To generate grounded dialogue, the structured scene representation must be connected to natural language via Large Language Models (LLMs). LLMs are built upon the Transformer architecture, which fundamentally relies on the self-attention mechanism~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. Rather than processing text sequentially like recurrent neural networks, self-attention allows the model to compute a weighted representation of every word in a sequence relative to all other words simultaneously. This mechanism enables the network to capture long-range semantic dependencies and contextual nuances, making autoregressive next-token prediction exceptionally coherent and fluent.

However, a base autoregressive model is optimized merely to predict the statistical continuation of a text corpus, which does not inherently make it a useful conversational agent. To function in a dialogue system, these base models undergo Instruction Tuning and alignment processes---often utilizing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)~\cite{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow}. This alignment transforms the model from a simple text-completer into an instruction-following agent capable of answering questions, declining unsafe requests, and maintaining a coherent conversational persona.
 

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) extend this capability by conditioning text generation on visual inputs~\cite{liu_visual_2023, dai_instructblip_2023, driess_palm-e_2023}. Typically, a Vision Encoder (such as a ViT) extracts features from an image, which are then projected into the LLM's embedding space. To the LLM, the image functions as a sequence of ``visual words'' appended to the text prompt.

As models scale to achieve better reasoning, computational costs rise dramatically. A dense model activates all its parameters for every token. To mitigate this, modern architectures frequently employ a Mixture of Experts (MoE) routing mechanism~\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks}. In an MoE model, only a small subset of specialized neural ``experts'' is activated for any given token. Consequently, if a system possesses sufficient RAM or VRAM to load the model weights, it can achieve the reasoning capabilities of a massive parameter model at the inference speed and compute cost of a much smaller model. This architectural efficiency is highly advantageous in robotics, where rapid inference is critical.


The frontier of multimodal AI is moving toward models that natively integrate spatial localization into their text generation. Kosmos-2~\cite{peng_kosmos-2_2023}, for example, represents spatial referring expressions as explicit Markdown links tied to bounding box coordinates, establishing a direct convergence between language generation and world modeling.

Despite their capabilities, VLMs are prone to hallucinating relations or failing to disambiguate multiple instances of the same object. To enforce precise visual grounding, this thesis utilizes Set-of-Mark (SoM) prompting~\cite{yang_set-of-mark_2023}. By rendering explicit visual markers---such as numbered bounding boxes or segmentation masks~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023}---directly onto the image, SoM transforms visual regions into explicit, referable symbols. This forces the VLM to anchor its relational reasoning to specific, tracked detections.

  \section{Language modeling:\@ LLMs, VLMs}

  In this system, language models connect machine perception to dialogue. Perception modules produce structured representations (detected objects, attributes, scene-graph relations, and memory
  summaries), and language models convert these structures into responses that are understandable, concise, and contextually appropriate for human-robot interaction. This is not only a text generation
  problem: the model output must remain faithful to current scene state and to persistent memory.

  Large language models (LLMs) are based on the Transformer architecture, where self-attention computes token-to-token dependencies across the whole context window \cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. In
  practice, this means the model does not process text as a strict recurrent chain. Instead, each new token is generated from a representation that already encodes long-range dependencies in the prompt
  and conversation history. The operational outcome is strong fluency and coherence in multi-turn dialogue, which is essential for a social robot that must keep consistent wording and references over
  time.

  A base autoregressive model is optimized for next-token prediction, not for reliable instruction following. Instruction-tuned models add an alignment stage that teaches the model to follow user intent
  and produce safer, more useful responses. InstructGPT-style alignment combines supervised fine-tuning with preference optimization through a reward model and reinforcement learning
  \cite{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow}. For this thesis, that distinction matters: the dialogue module requires instruction-following behavior (grounded answers, bounded style, and schema-
  constrained outputs), not open-ended continuation.

  Vision-language models (VLMs) extend the same principle to multimodal input. A vision encoder maps image content to latent features, those features are projected into the language model token space,
  and the decoder then generates text conditioned on both visual and textual context. LLaVA demonstrates this design by connecting a vision encoder to an LLM and instruction-tuning on visual dialogue-
  style data \cite{liu_visual_2023}. InstructBLIP adds an instruction-aware query transformer that selects visual features relevant to the current prompt before passing them to the language model
  \cite{dai_instructblip_2023}. PaLM-E further shows how an embodied model can ingest continuous sensor observations and robot-state signals as part of a unified token stream for robotic reasoning
  \cite{driess_palm-e_2023}. These models differ in architecture, but they share the same core mechanism: visual evidence is translated into language-model-compatible representations.

  Model scale introduces a latency-cost problem for robotics. Dense models activate all parameters for every token, which increases compute and response time. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) addresses this by
  routing each token through a small subset of experts while keeping total model capacity high \cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks}. For interactive HRI, this trade-off is practical: larger
  effective capacity can be used without paying full dense-model inference cost at every decoding step.

  A second practical issue is visual grounding precision. VLMs can produce fluent but weakly grounded outputs when multiple similar objects are present. Set-of-Mark prompting addresses this by drawing
  explicit visual indices or regions directly onto the image, so the model can refer to concrete marked entities instead of implicit regions \cite{yang_set-of-mark_2023}. Segment Anything provides
  promptable masks that can be used to create these explicit visual references \cite{kirillov_segment_2023}. Related multimodal work such as Kosmos-2 also formalizes grounding by linking text spans to
  spatial regions, reinforcing the same idea that language should bind to specific image locations \cite{peng_kosmos-2_2023}.

  Within this thesis pipeline, LLMs and VLMs are assigned distinct roles. The LLM is used for grounded dialogue generation over structured memory and for controlled generation tasks such as concise
  question-answer pairs. The VLM is used where semantic interpretation of visual content is needed beyond pure geometry. This separation keeps deterministic geometric reasoning in symbolic modules while
  reserving generative capacity for linguistic compression and higher-level semantic interpretation.

  Recent Pepper-focused studies support this design direction. Retrieval-augmented Pepper assistants have been reported for industrial support scenarios \cite{brad_retrieval-augmented_2025}. Educational
  and interaction-oriented prototypes combine Pepper with modern perception and language stacks, including YOLO+LLM integrations and object-aware storytelling
  \cite{latif_physicsassistant_2024,zabala_exploring_2025,wang_target_2024,becerra_improving_2024}. The common pattern is that language quality alone is insufficient: perceived usefulness depends on
  whether responses remain grounded in what the robot can actually perceive and do.

  The same trend appears in embodied planning literature. SayCan shows how language-model suggestions can be filtered by action feasibility scores before execution \cite{ahn_as_2022}. Code as Policies
  demonstrates direct synthesis of executable control logic from language instructions \cite{liang_code_2023}. Early humanoid experiments also explore LLM-mediated motion generation pipelines for NAO-
  like platforms \cite{catalini_llms_nodate}. For this thesis, these works motivate a conservative integration strategy: use language models as high-level semantic interfaces, but keep grounding,
  constraints, and execution checks explicit in the surrounding robotic system.
 



\section{RAG, CAG, and GraphRAG}


In exploring intelligent dialogue management for humanoid robots, the work of Grassi et al. provides a foundational perspective. Grassi et al.~\cite{grassi2022knowledge} demonstrated that managing the conversation flow via a structured ontology---rather than relying on purely generative or purely reactive keyword responses---significantly improves the subjective perception of coherence during interaction. Furthermore, Grassi et al.~\cite{grassi2023knowledge} highlighted the necessity of extracting and storing new knowledge from user interactions to dynamically expand the robot's conversational repertoire. Perhaps most importantly, Gava et al.~\cite{gava2024physical} empirically proved the value of the robotic form factor itself, showing that conversational agents deployed on physically embodied robots (like Pepper) elicit significantly higher user engagement and satisfaction than identical conversation frameworks deployed on smartphones. These findings underscore the premise of this thesis: generating fluent text is insufficient for embodied HRI; the dialogue must be grounded, structured, and situated within a physical robotic interaction.

Classical robotic dialogue systems historically rely on rigid, intent-based dialogue management architectures, such as Rasa~\cite{bocklisch_rasa_2017} or Pepper's native QiChat engine. While highly deterministic, these systems require developers to manually script every possible conversational branch, rendering them entirely incapable of handling open-ended questions about an unpredictable visual scene. Generative AI offers open-ended flexibility, provided the generation can be tethered to the robot's physical reality. In this thesis, the deterministic reliability of classical rule-based triggers (e.g., QiChat) is merged with the generative capacity of LLMs: the legacy engine routes user intent, while the LLM dynamically generates the grounded response.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis_retrieval-augmented_2020} provides a framework for enriching language models with external data. In modern AI architectures, executing RAG over multimodal data typically involves Multi-Vector Retrievers~\cite{noauthor_multi-vector_nodate}. Historically, this paradigm required passing visual modalities (images, tables) through a VLM to generate textual summaries, which were then embedded and stored in a vector database for standard semantic retrieval. While recent advancements by Google have introduced fully functional multimodal embedding spaces capable of natively indexing images and text concurrently~\cite{noauthor_multi-vector_nodate}, executing dense vector search remains computationally heavy. As Fazlollahtabar~\cite{fazlollahtabar_human-robot_2025} notes, integrating RAG with transformer networks is becoming a cornerstone of advanced Human-Robot Interaction.

However, the approach adopted in this thesis diverges from both probabilistic dense vector search and textual summary retrieval. By continuously updating a deterministic, symbolic representation---the Scene Graph---the system performs real-time structured extraction of the scene state. Because the scene representation is highly compact, it fits entirely within the LLM's context window. This transitions the pipeline from Retrieval-Augmented Generation to Cache-Augmented Generation (CAG)~\cite{Chan_2025}, eliminating the latency of vector search while guaranteeing absolute factual alignment with the robot's current perception.

Crucially, this structured approach shifts the cognitive burden placed on the LLM. Rather than relying on its internal Parametric Memory to hallucinate an answer, the model is tasked with ``reading comprehension'' over the injected context. By providing the exact physical state in the prompt, even relatively small LLMs can perform exceptionally well on knowledge tasks. Coupled with the MoE architectures discussed previously, this strategy drastically reduces VRAM requirements and inference latency, enabling highly responsive, factually grounded HRI on computationally constrained platforms.
