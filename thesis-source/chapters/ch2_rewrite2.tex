\section{Human-Robot Interaction and Social Robots}\label{sec:hri-social-robots}

%TL;DR: HRI is presented as a field that makes the quality of interaction part of the technical problem.
Human-robot interaction (HRI) studies the interaction of humans and robots, the design of these interactions and the systems that mediate them, and their evaluation. Goodrich and Schultz describe HRI as an interdisciplinary field. A good HRI system needs to integrate knowledge not only from robotics, but also from psychology and linguistics, since it must use techniques for successful interacting with humans~\cite[203--216]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}.

%TL;DR: Goodrich and Schultz list attributes for describing HRI systems.
From a design perspective, Goodrich and Schultz characterize an HRI problem using five attributes: the level of autonomy, the nature of the information exchange, the structure of the human-robot team, the adaptation and learning of both the human and the robot, and the shape of the task~\cite[216--217]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}.

%TL;DR: The five attributes define autonomy, communication, roles, adaptation, and task shape.
The level of autonomy determines the extent to which the robot acts independently and how control is distributed between the human and the robot. The nature of the information exchange includes characteristics of communication, such as modality, timing, and clarity. Team structure captures how the human-robot system is organized, including the number of participants and their roles (e.g., one human operating one robot or one human supervising a swarm of robots). Adaptation and learning describe how the human and/or robot adjust their behavior over time in response to their shared interaction. Task shape characterizes the task itself, or the structure of the problem being solved, including the nature of the goals, the degree of openness, and the constraints under which the interaction occurs~\cite[216--232]{goodrich_human-robot_2007}.

%TL;DR: Sheridan complements design attributes with application areas.
While these attributes fairly well describe how all HRI problems can be characterized, HRI can also be simply characterized as the study of situations in which robots and humans, in any number, engage in relationships and collaborations together. The next natural step is to examine HRI applications.

%TL;DR: Sheridan's taxonomy distinguishes social interaction from other areas of HRI.
Sheridan distinguishes four general domains of HRI applications: supervisory control, teleoperation, automated vehicles, and social interaction~\cite[525--526]{Sheridan2016HRI}. Supervisory control involves high-level human oversight of semi-autonomous systems. Teleoperation refers to direct remote control of a robot, typically in remote or hazardous environments (such as rescue operations). Automated vehicles operate largely autonomously, with humans acting either purely as passengers or as supervisors or backup operators. Social interaction focuses on robots whose primary function depends on direct communication and interaction with humans.

%TL;DR: Social robots originate from the field of social interaction.

The final category, social interaction, defines the domain in which social robots are found, thus linking the general formulations of HRI problems to social robotics systems.

The term [social robots] is not used uniformly in the literature. Henschel, Laban, and Cross note that there is no universally accepted definition, but that common characteristics include embodiment, some degree of autonomy, and interactional behavior that people interpret as social~\cite[11--12]{henschel_what_2021}.

%TL;DR: User studies identify the features that make robots appear to be social.
User studies further elaborate on what makes a robot social in practice. De Graaf et al. identified eight characteristics influencing perceived sociability: the ability to interact in two directions, the ability to express thoughts and feelings, awareness of the social environment, the provision of social support, autonomy, and to a lesser extent coziness, similarity to the user, and mutual respect~\cite{de_graaf_what_2015}. \section{Problem Setting: Scene-Aware Dialogue}\label{sec:problem-setting-scene-aware-dialogue}

%TL;DR: Scene-aware dialogue is defined as a system-level task.
Scene-aware dialogue, at least as defined in this thesis, refers to interaction in which the robot maintains a representation of its perceived environment and uses this representation to generate its responses.

Formally speaking, the system operates on a sequence of observations \(O_1, O_2, \ldots, O_T\), where each observation \(O_t\) consists of an image along with robot metadata (e.g., camera pose, head orientation, and other perception signals coming from NAOqi API).

From each observation, the system extracts information about the scene and updates the internal memory. The memory at time \(t\) can be written as
\[
M_t = (\mathcal{E}_t, \mathcal{R}_t, \mathcal{H}_t),
\]
where \(\mathcal{E}_t\) is a set of persistent entities (objects), \(\mathcal{R}_t\) represents relations between them (e.g., a scene graph), and \(\mathcal{H}_t\) stores history from past dialogue.


The memory is updated incrementally:
\[
M_t = U(M_{t-1}, O_t),
\]
where the update function \(U\) includes object detection and feature extraction from the observation \(O_t\), identity association (re-identification), relationship extraction, and integration into the existing memory.

Here it is explicitly seen that the system does not respond only based on the current observation, but also based on the accumulated representation of the scene. This achieves scene-aware dialogue.

At the dialogue level, in turn \(k\), the user issues a query \(q_k\). The system retrieves the relevant context \(C_k\) from memory and generates a response using the language model $p_\theta$:
\[
y_k \sim p_\theta(y \mid q_k, C_k).
\]

The main question in the design of a dialogue system is therefore how to construct \(C_k\). General questions (e.g. \enquote{what do you see?}) require a summary of the scene, while targeted questions (e.g. \enquote{what is next to the laptop?}) require the retrieval of a specific subset of entities and relations. LLM, RAG, and CAG are suitable solutions to this problem.

In short, the whole system consists of the following components: observation processing (image + metadata), object detection and feature extraction, identity comparison between observations, relationship extraction, memory update, context selection, and response generation. The following sections introduce these components in detail.

\section{Object Detection, Tracking, and Re-Identification}\label{sec:object-detection-tracking-reid}

%TL;DR: Object detection provides localized object hypotheses.
The first step, or technical requirement, for perceiving a scene is to see the individual objects. Object detection is the task of identifying object instances in an image and locating them, most often using bounding boxes.

Formally, a detector maps an image \(I\) to a finite set
\[
\mathcal{D}(I)=\{(b_i,\ell_i,s_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where \(b_i\in\mathbb{R}^4\) is the bounding box, \(\ell_i\) is the class label, and \(s_i\in[0,1]\) is the confidence score~\cite[Section 50.4.1]{foundationsCVbook}.

The traditional object detection procedure consists of three phases. First, the system proposes candidate bounding boxes. Second, each candidate region is classified, often with an additional step of refining the bounding box. Third, the resulting bounding boxes are then processed to remove low-confidence and redundant detections. Redundancy is addressed using overlap measures such as Intersection over Union (IoU), defined as the ratio of the area of intersection of two bounding boxes to the area of their union, along with methods such as non-maximum suppression (NMS), which preserves the box with the highest confidence while suppressing overlapping alternatives~\cite[Section 50.4.1.2]{foundationsCVbook}.

Modern object detection builds on the development of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which have been the main architecture for neural networks with image data. CNNs exploit the spatial structure of the data by applying learned local filters and reusing them at different locations in the image. This makes them suitable for visual data, since local patterns in this kind of data, such as edges or textures, appear in different places.

The first layers of a CNN typically represent simple local structures, while the deeper layers decompose these structures into more abstract elements that are useful for a subsequent task, such as recognition and detection~\cite[439]{lecun_deep_2015}.

In addition, CNNs have a huge impact on the history of neural networks. As Goodfellow et al. note, they were among the first deep models to achieve commercial success, including early check-reading and handwriting recognition systems, paving the way for other neural architectures~\cite[365]{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}.


Early deep learning detectors used CNNs within a traditional suggestion-based pipeline. In R-CNN, REDUNDANT INPHRASE THING one of the first successful neural object detection models, candidate regions are not generated by the CNN itself, but by selective search, an external, class-agnostic design method. It groups pixels into small segments and hierarchically connects similar neighboring regions based on low-level cues, i.e. shared visual properties such as color, texture, size, and spatial compatibility ~\cite{uijlings_selective_2013}. The resulting boxes are only hypotheses about the location of objects and do not yet determine object classes.

R-CNN then processes each proposed region independently by cropping, warping to a fixed size, extracting CNN features, and classifying them using a separate classifier~\cite{girshick_rich_2014}. This approach produced the first effective object detector based on deep learning, but was computationally intensive due to the repeated evaluation of the CNN for many regions. Fast R-CNN improved efficiency by computing convolutional features once for the entire image and merging region features from a shared feature map~\cite{girshick_fast_2015}.

%Faster R-CNN removed the external selective-search step by introducing a Region Proposal Network (RPN), a small fully convolutional network that slides over the shared feature map and predicts objectness scores and box offsets for predefined anchor boxes~\cite{ren_faster_2016}. These methods show the transition from hand-designed proposal pipelines toward learned detectors, but they still preserve a two-stage structure: propose regions, then classify and refine them.


YOLO, which stands for \emph{You Only Look Once}, takes a different, more up-to-date approach. Instead of the original path of generating many candidate regions and classifying them separately, YOLO processes the entire image in a single forward pass through a single neural network~\cite{redmon_you_2016}. This is the reason for the \enquote{look once}.

In the original formulation of YOLO, the image is first partitioned into a spatial grid. The convolutional part of the network extracts the image features, and each cell of the grid predicts the bounding boxes, confidence scores, and class probabilities for objects whose centers fall within that cell. In this way, localization, classification, and confidence of prediction are predicted together, in a single forward pass; the model directly predicts the boxes and object labels from the global representation of the image without first generating a separate list of candidate regions. Therefore, YOLO may be more computationally efficient than methods based on window scanning or suggestions~\cite[Sec.~50.4.1.4]{foundationsCVbook}\cite{redmon_you_2016}.

Modern YOLO models build on this efficiency, they also differ from the original architecture while still maintaining the conceptual structure. The \enquote{backbone} extracts visual features from an image. The \enquote{neck} combines features at multiple scales, which helps detect both small and large objects. The \enquote{head} detection predicts bounding box coordinates, objectness/confidence values, and class scores.

The predictions are then filtered by confidence and typically post-processed using an NMS, as the number of predicted boxes is huge. YOLO models from Ultralytics, are a prominent and widely used modern implementation family, released in various sizes to balance accuracy against latency and computational cost. Their use is well documented and ranges from robotics to agriculture, clearly demonstrating their practicality~\cite[1, 4, 10--11]{sapkota_ultralytics_2026}. CAN WE MOVE HALF TO IMPLEMENTATION?

Another paradigm shift in object detection is transformer-based object detection. DETR, the detection transformer, formulates detection as a direct prediction of set~\cite{carion_end--end_2020}. The image backbone first extracts visual features. The transformer encoder processes these features using self-attention, and the transformer decoder uses the learned object queries to produce a fixed-size set of object predictions.\footnote{For a formal definition of self-attention and transformer, see Section \ref{sec:llms-vlms}.}

This means that the model does not produce an ordered list of dense local predictions that must later be cleaned by NMS.
Each query predicts either one object or a special no-object class. During training, predictions are matched to ground-truth objects with bipartite matching, which encourages one prediction per object and reduces the need for NMS.


The difference between YOLO and DETR is therefore structural. YOLO is a dense, one-stage detector: spatial locations in feature maps directly predict the frames and class scores. DETR is a set-based detector: learned queries predict a set of hypothesis objects.

YOLO excels in real-time scenarios due to its efficient formulation of dense prediction, but it relies on post-processing such as non-maximization suppression and primarily uses local reasoning about features. DETR, in contrast, uses global attention and formulates detection as a set prediction problem, which simplifies the process and can better capture the relationships between objects and context, but it typically requires more computation and is harder to train. As a result, YOLO is often preferred in latency-critical applications, while DETR-style models are preferable when global reasoning and architectural simplicity are more important. Object detection, be it open or closed-vocabulary, is always local to a given image frame; It produces detections for one image, but it does not by itself determine whether an object in frame \(t\) corresponds to the same physical instance in frame \(t-1\). Multi-object tracking (MOT) addresses this problem by connecting observations across time.

In general, MOT can be thought of as a state estimation problem over a sequence. Let \(s_i^t\) denote the state of object \(i\) at time \(t\), such as its position, size, velocity, and identity.
Let \(S_t\) denote the set of all object states in frame \(t\), and let \(S_{1:t}\) denote the sequence of such state sets from the first frame up to frame \(t\). Similarly, let \(O_t\) denote the observations available in frame \(t\), and \(O_{1:t}\) the sequence of observations over time. In the common tracking-by-detection formulation,\footnote{There is also a \emph{tracking-by-query} formulation, which is not discussed here.} these observations are typically produced by an object detector, for example as detections
\[
\mathcal{D}_t = \{(b_i^t, \ell_i^t, c_i^t)\}_{i=1}^{N_t},
\]
where \(b_i^t\) is a bounding box, \(\ell_i^t\) is a class label, and \(c_i^t\) is a confidence score.

The objective of MOT is to estimate the most likely sequence of object states given the observations. This can be expressed as a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation problem,
\[
\hat{S}_{1:t} = \arg\max_{S_{1:t}} P(S_{1:t} \mid O_{1:t}),
\]
where \(\hat{S}_{1:t}\) is the estimated sequence of object states. Practical methods approximate this objective through sequential prediction and association~\cite{luo_multiple_2021}. %In the common \emph{tracking-by-detection} formulation,detections are linked across frames to form object tracks.

A common baseline is SORT~\cite{bewley_simple_2016}, which combines a Kalman filter for motion prediction with Hungarian assignment based on bounding-box overlap (IoU). While this approach is efficient, it relies exclusively on geometry and short-term motion. Deep SORT~\cite{wojke_simple_2017} extends this formulation by introducing an appearance model: each detection is mapped to an embedding, and association considers both spatial proximity and visual similarity. This reduces identity switching in cases where objects intersect or are partially occluded, as the tracker can rely on appearance cues in addition to motion.

ByteTrack addresses another limitation of detection-based tracking channels. Many tracking devices discard low-confidence detections before association, which can fragment tracks when the detector's reliability drops due to overlap or blurring. Instead, ByteTrack performs association in two stages: first comparing high-confidence detections, and then attempting to associate the remaining tracks with lower-confidence detections~\cite{zhang_bytetrack_2022}. This simple modification improves robustness to temporary detection failures without introducing additional appearance modeling, highlighting a complementary strategy to methods such as Deep SORT.

Despite these differences, detection-based tracking methods share a common assumption of temporal and spatial continuity. Objects are expected to move smoothly, remain visible in successive frames, and appear in close locations within the frame. When these assumptions hold, motion, overlap, and short-term appearance cues provide reliable cues for association. When they are violated—due to overlap, sudden changes in viewpoint, or sparse observations—tracking becomes unreliable, and identities can be lost.

This motivates re-identification as a related but distinct problem. Re-identification does not attempt to estimate a continuous frame-by-frame trajectory. Instead, it asks whether two observations correspond to the same physical instance at different settings. Zheng and Zheng define object re-identification as the recognition of object instances, not just object categories, in different contexts of time, location, or viewpoint~\cite{zheng_object_2024}. This distinction is important: comparing \enquote{the same chair} is different from detecting that both observations contain \enquote{a chair}.

Re-ID systems therefore focus on representation learning. The embedding should preserve instance-specific visual cues while being robust to nuisance variation such as viewpoint, illumination, scale, resolution, partial occlusion, and background change. Classical methods used hand-crafted descriptors and metric learning; modern methods usually use deep networks trained with identification losses, contrastive losses, triplet losses, or combinations of these objectives~\cite{zheng_object_2024}. The goal is not to classify an object into a category, but to place observations of the same instance close together in feature space.

Re-identification is not a complete replacement for tracking. It does not provide what tracking yealds, that is, a smooth trajectory or velocity estimate. However, it is a suitable substitute for the identity-association part when the assumptions of continuous tracking are violated. In a continuous video, SORT, Deep SORT, or ByteTrack can maintain identities using motion and short-term association. \section{Language Models and Vision-Language Models}\label{sec:llms-vlms}

%TL;DR: Language modeling is probabilistic sequence modeling.
Natural language processing (NLP) is the computational study and processing of human language. Language modeling is a narrower task within NLP: estimating probabilities over sequences of tokens. For a token sequence \(x_{1:T}\), an autoregressive language model factors
\[
p(x_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_t\mid x_{<t}).
\]
Generation is thus the repeated prediction of the next token. This definition is important because it avoids a common misunderstanding: a language model is optimized to produce probable text in context, NOTE: not to be true by construction, so RAG.

One of the simplest language modeling approach is the n-gram model. It estimates the probability of the next token from a fixed number of previous tokens. For example, a trigram model approximates \(p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\) by conditioning only on \(x_{t-2}\) and \(x_{t-1}\). This makes the model simple and efficient, but it also introduces a severe restriction on context; Information outside a fixed window is ignored. Increasing \(n\) gives the model more context, but also causes the number of possible token combinations to grow rapidly, leading to data sparsity.

A widely adopted models for various NLP tasks were the recurrent neural networks (RNNs). They remove the fixed-window assumption by processing a sequence through a hidden state,
\[
h_t = f_\theta(h_{t-1}, x_t),
\]
so that information from previous tokens can in principle be carried forward through \(h_t\). The hidden state acts as a compressed summary of the previous context. This is more flexible than an n-gram window, but it also has a bottleneck of long-range information overwriting or dilution. Gated variants such as LSTMs and GRUs improve this mechanism by learning when to write, forget, or expose parts of the state, partly addressing the vanishing-gradient problem. However, recurrence has also one computationally critical problem, it is sequential. Token \(t\) depends on the computed state from token \(t-1\). This limits parallel training on long sequences and became a major practical disadvantage once transformer models showed strong scaling behavior \parencites[441--442]{lecun_deep_2015}[370, 372--376]{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}..

The idea of sequential models with latent state has not gone away completely. Recent stateful models, such as Mamba, are returning in this direction, using selective state updates and hardware-aware parallel algorithms~\cite{gu_mamba_2023}. This shows that alternatives to attention remain active research directions. However, the dominant architecture for current large language models remains Transformer.

%TL;DR: Transformers replace recurrence with attention plus position information.
The Transformer architecture replaces recurrence with self-at\-ten\-tion~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. For hidden states \(X\), scaled dot-product attention can be written as
\[
\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)
=
\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V,
\]
where \(Q=XW_Q\), \(K=XW_K\), and \(V=XW_V\) are learned projections, \(d_k\) is the key dimension, and \(M\) is an optional mask. In autoregressive language modeling, the mask prevents a token from attending to future tokens.

Self-attention allows each token to directly attend to other tokens in the context. Unlike recurrence, this makes training highly parallel because hidden states for many positions can be computed at the same time. A pure attention layer, however, does not inherently know token order: without additional information, it treats the input as a set of tokens. Transformers therefore add positional information, either through fixed positional encodings, learned positional embeddings, or later variants such as relative or rotary position encodings. In this way, the model gains access to order while retaining the parallelism of attention~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}.

Modern LLMs are usually not the whole original architecture but rather decoder-only Transformer models trained with an autoregressive next-token prediction objective.\@ Earlier sequence-to-sequence Transformer systems used separate encoder and decoder stacks, especially for tasks such as machine translation.\@ Decoder-only models instead condition each generated token on the previous tokens in a single causal context.\@ When scaled to large datasets and model sizes, this objective produces models that can perform many tasks through prompting, although this behavior should be understood as conditional generation from context rather than guaranteed reasoning or truthfulness. Instruction tuning and alignment methods further adapt pretrained models for dialogue and instruction following~\cite{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow}.

\section{RAG, CAG, and GraphRAG}\label{sec:rag-cag-graphrag}

%TL;DR: RAG separates language generation from external evidence access.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines a generator with external evidence retrieval. Lewis et al. describe RAG as a combination of parametric memory in a pretrained model and non-parametric memory in a retrievable corpus~\cite{lewis_retrieval-augmented_2020}. Given a query, a retriever selects relevant context, and the generator produces an answer conditioned on both the query and that context.

%TL;DR: Retrieval quality introduces precision-latency tradeoffs.
In many RAG systems, retrieval is embedding-based. A query and document chunks are mapped into a vector space, and relevance is approximated by cosine similarity or inner product. Approximate nearest-neighbor indexing makes retrieval efficient for large corpora, but it introduces tradeoffs between speed, recall, embedding dimensionality, chunking, and reranking. In interaction, these tradeoffs affect both answer quality and response latency.

%TL;DR: Chunking determines what evidence can later be retrieved.
A central design choice in RAG is chunking: the source material must be divided into retrieval units before it can be indexed. Simple strategies use fixed-length token windows, sentence-level chunks, or paragraph-level chunks. More adaptive strategies use semantic boundaries, document structure, or model-assisted chunking. The chunk size controls a tradeoff. Small chunks can improve retrieval precision, but they may lose surrounding context. Large chunks preserve more context, but they may retrieve irrelevant material and consume more of the model context window. Contextual retrieval addresses this problem by enriching each chunk with additional document-level context before embedding or indexing it, so that the chunk remains interpretable even when retrieved in isolation~\cite{noauthor_contextual_nodate}. Thus, chunking is not only a storage detail; it determines what facts and relations can be recovered at generation time.


%TL;DR: RAG is useful for changing knowledge, but naive chunking can destroy structure.
RAG is generally useful when the model needs external knowledge or access to a constantly changing knowledge base. In scene-aware dialogue, however, the external knowledge is not a normal document collection. It is the robot's current and past scene state. A naive textual RAG approach could serialize the scene graph into chunks, but chunking can destroy the relations that make the graph useful. If \enquote{cup}, \enquote{table}, and \enquote{on} are separated, the generator receives fragments rather than the scene fact \((\text{cup},\text{on},\text{table})\). Even if the relation is not separated, retrieving it as plain text loses the graph neighborhood around the involved objects. This motivates retrieval mechanisms that preserve structured entities, attributes, and relations rather than treating scene memory as ordinary prose.

%TL;DR: Multimodal RAG generalizes retrieval beyond text.
Recent retrieval-augmented work also extends beyond text into images and multimodal data~\cite{zheng_retrieval_2025}. Older multimodal RAG pipelines often converted images or other modalities into a primary textual representation and retrieved over that representation~\cite[2--4]{mao_multi-rag_2025}. Newer systems can retrieve across text, image, table, audio, video, or other representations using multimodal embeddings.\footnote{For example, recently released Google's Gemini Embedding 2 maps text, images, documents, audio, and video into a unified embedding space, illustrating the practical availability of multimodal embedding models: \url{https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/models/gemini/embedding-2}.} This is relevant background, although scene-aware dialogue often needs structured retrieval over entities and relations rather than only semantic similarity.

%TL;DR: CAG preloads bounded knowledge into context.
Cache-Augmented Generation (CAG) uses a different assumption. Instead of retrieving external chunks at every turn, it preloads relevant knowledge into the model context or cache and reuses it during generation. Chan et al. argue that, for some bounded knowledge tasks, CAG can be sufficient and can avoid RAG's online retrieval overhead~\cite{Chan_2025}. The prerequisite is that the relevant knowledge fits into the usable context and does not change too rapidly. This is, however, nowdays made possible by the ever increasing context window of LLMs.

%TL;DR: CAG moves selection from query time to cache construction time.

In CAG, the system decides what to include when constructing the cache. This is useful for broad scene awareness, but less useful when a question requires precise retrieval around one object or relation.

%TL;DR: GraphRAG retrieves through relational structure.
GraphRAG introduces explicit relational structure into retrieval. Instead of treating memory as independent chunks, it represents memory as a graph and retrieves evidence through traversal, neighborhood expansion, or graph summaries. This is natural when the source representation is already relational. A question about an object can retrieve its local neighborhood rather than an unrelated textual chunk.

This is especially important for scene-aware dialogs, because the memory state is already graph-like. Objects are nodes, attributes are node properties, and relationships are edges. A query like \enquote{what's on the table?} should retrieve the table node and its neighboring \enquote{on} relationships, not just the text block that is semantically most similar to the query. Graph-based search therefore matches the memory structure of the scene more directly than regular vector search. Its limitation is that it depends on the quality of the graph: missed detections, incorrect identities, or incorrect relationships propagate into the search.

Such grammar- or phrase-based interaction is useful for bounded commands, but it does not by itself provide open-ended grounded dialogue.

%TL;DR: Pepper's tablet is part of the interaction channel.
Pepper's tablet is not merely an attached display. It is an interaction modality that can show options, present visual state, or support disambiguation~\cite{softbank_peppers_tablet_2026}. In grounded dialogue, this matters because visible feedback can reduce the mismatch between the robot's internal state and the user's interpretation.

%TL;DR: Existing Pepper systems commonly rely on external computation.
Several related Pepper systems already use external processing or modern AI components. Reyes et al. demonstrate near real-time object recognition for Pepper using deep neural networks running outside the robot~\cite{reyes_near_2018}. Trinquet et al. combine Pepper with object detection and OCR for library assistance~\cite{trinquet_future_2025}. Brad et al. propose a RAG architecture for Pepper in industrial assistance~\cite{brad_retrieval-augmented_2025}. Latif et al. build an LLM-powered interactive learning robot with YOLO-based perception~\cite{latif_physicsassistant_2024}.

%TL;DR: The platform motivates distributed scene-aware dialogue.
These works show that distributed architectures are a recurring response to Pepper's computational limits. They also show that Pepper is a useful platform for studying grounded interaction because it forces the full stack to be considered: sensing, dialogue, latency, embodiment, user expectations, and external computation. The specific implementation choices are therefore described later as architectural responses to these platform constraints.
