\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Social robots, that is, robots designed for interaction, communication, and assistance in human-centered environments, are becoming increasingly present in everyday life. They are already deployed in domains such as healthcare, assisted living, education, public services, and industry. As their deployment broadens, human-robot interaction (HRI) is no longer a purely theoretical field but an increasingly practical concern.

Sheridan's well-known taxonomy divides HRI into supervisory control, teleoperation, automated vehicles, and social interaction~\cite[525-526]{Sheridan2016HRI}. This thesis focuses on the latter category, where robots are expected to assist, teach, comfort, or accompany users through direct social engagement. While the focus in this thesis is on social interaction, the underlying architectural principles, particularly the integration of perception, memory, and language, are likely to be relevant across other HRI domains mentioned in Sheridan's classification as well.

The importance of socially capable robots is especially visible in healthcare. HRI in this domain is inherently interdisciplinary, combining engineering, social sciences, and design to create systems that can interact with humans in meaningful ways. Robots are being explored as monitoring tools, rehabilitation assistants, guides, and providers of emotional support~\cite{Han2024HRIHealthcare}. Applications extend to autism therapy and education, where robots such as NAO and Pepper by Aldebaran, two robots most dominantly used in HRI research, have demonstrated promising, although still not convincing, results~\cite{Tapus2012AutismNao, aturhurra_leveraging_2024}. More broadly, recent literature and medicine practice emphasizes that social interaction is not merely an auxiliary feature, but a component that can directly affect the overall care quality, thus imposing significant requirements for any HRI systems in medicine to behave safely and adapt to user context~\cite{Han2024HRIHealthcare}.

Empirical studies further illustrate both the promise and the challenges of these systems. In dementia care, stakeholders report clear benefits of Pepper in reminders, safety monitoring, and social stimulation, while also raising concerns about trust, reliability, privacy, and personalization~\cite{liao_use_2023}. Outside healthcare, similar patterns emerge. Pepper has been explored as an intelligent assistant in libraries using visual perception and OCR, and multimodal assistants combining vision with large language models have been proposed for educational environments such as physics laboratories~\cite{trinquet_future_2025, latif_physicsassistant_2024}. Across these applications, a consistent conclusion emerges: the usefulness of a social robot does not lie in speech alone or in any of its systems in isolation, but in its ability to connect language or other systems to the situation in which the interaction occurs.

This observation naturally leads to the concept of scene grounding. For interaction to be meaningful, a robot must not only generate fluent responses, but also relate them to its current perception, for example through identifying relevant objects and people, maintaining awareness over time, and grounding its responses in the observed environment. 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) provide a powerful mechanism for such interaction, effectively replacing traditionally rather modular HRI pipelines, such as automatic speech recognition, intent detection, dialogue management, and text-to-speech (TTS), with a unified generative framework~\cite{aturhurra_leveraging_2024}. This enables more natural and flexible interaction, but also amplifies the need for grounding, as users increasingly expect responses to reflect the robot's actual situational awareness. 

It is evident LLM-based systems improve conversational richness and engagement, but this in turn also raises expectations regarding contextual awareness and appropriate behavior~\cite{kim_understanding_2024}. Atuhurra further highlights that integrating LLMs and vision-language models (VLMs) into socially assistive robotics introduces both opportunities and risks, particularly in terms of trust, bias, and responsible deployment~\cite{aturhurra_leveraging_2024}. Together, these developments highlight a central limitation: fluent language without grounding leads to a mismatch between conversational ability, and real-world understanding, resulting in an unfullfilled human information need. 



From a technical perspective, this challenge is tightly coupled with the need to bridge perception and language. 
Modern AI systems for object detection, visual understanding, and multimodal reasoning operate largely as separate components. 
However, meaningful interaction in embodied settings requires these components to be integrated into a coherent pipeline. 
In particular, representing the environment in a structured form, such as a scene graph capturing objects, attributes, and relationships, provides a critical intermediate layer between low-level perception and high-level reasoning~\cite{johnson_image_2015}. 
Such structured representations enable relational reasoning, support more complex queries, and form a basis for consistent communication about the environment.
 
Another key requirement is persistence. Real-world interaction is not limited to a single observation; it unfolds over time. A robot must therefore maintain a form of memory that tracks objects across frames, stores attributes and relationships, and allows the system to answer questions not only about the current scene but also about recent context. This aligns with broader trends in robotics research, where higher-level competence is increasingly associated with persistent, semantically rich scene representations rather than purely geometric or frame-based perception~\cite{mascaro_scene_2025, peng_object_2024}.

For the Pepper robot, these challenges are further constrained by hardware limitations. State-of-the-art models for detection, captioning, multimodal reasoning, and LLMs are computationally demanding and cannot be executed efficiently on Pepper's onboard hardware. 
As a result, modern systems rely on distributed architectures in which perception and reasoning are offloaded to external compute resources. This pattern has been observed consistently in prior work: near real-time perception has been achieved using external edge hardware~\cite{reyes_near_2018, wilson_enhancing_2025}, industrial assistant systems distribute computation between local and remote resources~\cite{becerra_improving_2024, brad_retrieval-augmented_2025, hafez_enhancing_nodate}, and visually grounded conversational systems combine Pepper with external captioning and language models~\cite{grassi_grounding_2024, latif_physicsassistant_2024, trinquet_future_2025}. These approaches demonstrate that off-board computation is not merely an implementation detail, but a fundamental architectural requirement for enabling advanced capabilities on current robotic platforms which are computationally limited.

The motivation of this thesis is therefore to move beyond isolated perception or language capabilities and toward a unified system that enables scene-aware dialogue. In this context, scene-aware dialogue refers to the ability of a robot to perceive its environment, identify and track relevant entities, represent their attributes and relationships, maintain this information over time, and use it as a basis for coherent, grounded conversation.

In summary, this thesis is motivated by the need to integrate perception, memory, and language into a coherent framework for embodied AI.\@ By combining modern computer vision, structured scene representations, persistent memory, and conversational models within a distributed architecture, the work aims to advance social robots toward more context-aware, reliable, and meaningful interaction in real-world environments.


\section{Problem Statement and Objectives}
Having outlined the fundamental limitation of non-grounded human-robot interaction, it becomes necessary to move toward systems that explicitly integrate perception, memory, and language into grounded interaction.
Thus the central problem addressed in this thesis is how to enable the Pepper robot to perceive its surroundings, represent them in a structured and persistent form, and use this representation as the basis for coherent scene-aware dialogue with the user.
Where by a scene-aware dialogue we understand the ability to identify relevant objects and people, track them across observations, represent their attributes and relationships, integrate robot-native perception signals, and use this evolving state to answer follow-up questions in a consistent and context-aware manner using RAG techniques and LLMs.

To address this problem, the thesis defines the following objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Design a system architecture that enables the use of modern detection and language models despite the computational limitations of the robot platform.
    \item Develop a perception pipeline that preserves object identity across time through multi-object tracking.
    \item Construct a structured representation of the scene, capturing objects, their attributes, and relationships.
    \item Integrate server-side perception with robot-native sensing (e.g., NAOqi APIs), particularly for human-related and social signals.
    \item Enable dialogue generation grounded in the current and recent scene states, including support for multi-turn and multilingual interaction (namely Czech and English).
    \item Provide mechanisms for monitoring, configuration, and evaluation of the system, including assessment of scene representation quality and interaction behavior.
\end{itemize}

The main approach taken in this thesis stems from several ideas of prior work. The overall concept of grounding dialogue in visual scene understanding is inspired by the system proposed by Grassi et al.~\cite{grassi_grounding_2024}. The use of external computation to overcome hardware limitations, specifically for the Pepper platform, follows established approaches based on edge or remote, cloud processing~\cite{reyes_near_2018, trinquet_future_2025, brad_retrieval-augmented_2025}. Additional design decisions and components draw on a broader set of related work, which will be discussed in detail in the corresponding implementation sections in this thesis.

\section{Research Questions}
The problem formulation leads to these five research questions. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{System architecture:} How can Pepper's limited onboard capabilities be extended through a remote multimodal inference server while preserving responsiveness and maintaining grounded interaction?

    \item \textbf{Scene representation:} How should object detection, persistent tracking, and scene graph generation be combined to form a dynamic world model suitable for dialogue?

    \item \textbf{Multimodal fusion:} What benefits, if any, arise from integrating Pepper-native people perception and social cues with server-side visual detections?

    \item \textbf{Scene graph methods:} To what extent can a hybrid scene graph approach, combining rule-based reasoning, vision-language model prompting, and learned relation prediction, support grounded dialogue?

    \item \textbf{Robustness:} How sensitive is scene graph quality to factors such as prompt design, vocabulary size, and model or provider choice?
\end{enumerate}


\section{Main Contributions}
The primary contribution of this thesis is the design and implementation of a scene-aware dialogue system for the Pepper robot that integrates embodied interaction with server-side multimodal inference and explicit scene memory.

On the server side, it implements a configurable FastAPI-based system capable of performing multimodal inference (e.g., text and image processing, and text generation), maintaining scene memory, and supporting multiple interaction modes, including captioning, detection, and memory-grounded dialogue.

\chapter{Background and Related Work}

\section{Social Robotics and Human-Robot Interaction}
Social robots operate in shared human environments and are therefore judged by criteria that go beyond raw task completion. In addition to functional competence, users expect legible behavior, timely responses, awareness of nearby people and objects, and interaction that remains intelligible even when perception is imperfect. These requirements make embodiment and situatedness central design constraints rather than cosmetic additions. Recent HRI literature on large language model integration argues that LLMs raise user expectations even further: once a robot speaks fluently, users assume it also understands the physical context, social situation, and consequences of its utterances \cite{kim_understanding_2024, atuhurra_leveraging_2024}.

Pepper is a particularly relevant platform in this context because it is explicitly designed for front-facing interaction, speech, and social presence rather than high-payload manipulation. That design makes it a strong candidate for scene-aware dialogue research, but it also exposes the limits of conventional scripted interaction. A robot that can speak naturally but cannot reliably refer to people, objects, or spatial relations in its immediate surroundings creates a mismatch between conversational fluency and situated understanding. This thesis addresses that mismatch by treating perception-grounded world modeling as a first-class requirement of dialogue rather than as a peripheral sensing add-on.

\subsection{Grounded Interaction in HRI}
In robotics, grounding denotes the connection between linguistic expressions and the physical entities, relations, and events to which those expressions refer. A purely text-based assistant may answer questions from prior knowledge alone, but a robot embedded in a physical environment must resolve references such as ``the person on my left,'' ``the bottle near the laptop,'' or ``the object you just showed me'' against the current scene. Grounding therefore depends on perceptual access, state maintenance, and the ability to preserve referential continuity over time.

This distinction is particularly important in social HRI because dialogue unfolds under uncertainty and in the presence of changing scenes. The robot must manage deictic language, partial occlusion, and conversational references to previously observed objects while remaining responsive. Grassi et al.\ present a close system-level precedent by combining dense captioning with large language models to support visually grounded Pepper interactions \cite{grassi_grounding_2024}. Their work demonstrates that Pepper can sustain vision-based conversation when scene information is converted into textual descriptions. However, the representation remains largely caption-centric. The present thesis extends this direction toward persistent object identities, structured scene memory, explicit inter-object relations, and configurable scene graph generation, which together support more stable reference resolution and richer scene-aware dialogue.

\subsection{Pepper Robot and the NAOqi Ecosystem}
Pepper provides a mature interaction platform with integrated cameras, microphones, loudspeakers, a tablet, and a broad set of NAOqi services. The software stack is historically rooted in the NAOqi Python SDK, which in the relevant deployment line remains tied to Python 2.7 and robot-specific middleware constraints \cite{softbank_python_sdk_2026}. NAOqi also exposes speech and multimodal interaction services such as robot-native speech recognition and tablet output, both of which remain practically useful in deployed interaction pipelines \cite{softbank_alspeechrecognition_2026, softbank_peppers_tablet_2026}. These platform characteristics strongly shape the architecture of any modern Pepper system: the robot is well suited to embodied interaction, but contemporary vision and language models generally need to run off-board.

This trade-off is well documented in prior Pepper vision systems. Reyes et al.\ already showed that near real-time object recognition required auxiliary hardware because Pepper's onboard computer was insufficient for contemporary deep neural inference \cite{reyes_near_2018}. More recent Pepper systems that integrate LLMs, retrieval, or modern computer vision continue to rely on split architectures in which perception and reasoning are externalized while robot-native services remain responsible for sensing access, speech, and embodied delivery \cite{brad_retrieval-augmented_2025}. The architecture implemented in this thesis follows the same general engineering pattern, but with a stronger emphasis on structured world modeling, person-aware fusion, and scene-grounded response generation.

\section{Dialogue Systems for Robots}
Classical dialogue systems typically separate natural language understanding, dialogue management, and language generation into distinct components. This modular view remains useful because it clarifies where state is maintained and how interaction policy is chosen \cite{chen_survey_2017, bocklisch_rasa_2017}. In robotics, however, dialogue management cannot be reduced to intent-slot filling alone. The system must also consider perception delays, uncertainty in speech recognition, turn-taking under embodiment, and grounding to the current physical scene. Rule-based managers offer control and predictability, but they become brittle when users move beyond predefined flows or refer to novel objects and relations.

LLM-based dialogue broadens linguistic coverage and improves robustness to paraphrase, underspecification, and unanticipated questions. The cost is that fluent responses may outstrip the reliability of the underlying world model. In HRI, this is not a minor defect: users can interpret verbal confidence as situated competence, which amplifies the risks of hallucination, overclaiming, or socially inappropriate behavior \cite{kim_understanding_2024, atuhurra_leveraging_2024}. For this reason, the present thesis does not treat the LLM as a free-form conversational oracle. Instead, it positions the model as a language generation layer operating over explicit scene state, recent perception summaries, and tracked conversational history.

\subsection{Conversation Memory and Context Injection}
A useful distinction in grounded dialogue is the one between conversational memory and world-state memory. Conversational memory stores prior utterances, clarifications, and interaction goals. World-state memory stores what the robot currently believes about objects, people, attributes, and spatial relations. These two forms of memory overlap during dialogue, but they serve different roles: the first preserves discourse continuity, while the second preserves referential continuity.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation provides the standard conceptual framework for enriching language model outputs with external information \cite{lewis_retrieval-augmented_2020}. More recent work on tool use and self-correction strengthens the broader point that LLM reliability improves when the model can draw on external state and feedback rather than relying exclusively on parametric memory \cite{schick_toolformer_2023, gou_critic_2023}. The system implemented in this thesis should therefore be understood as a lightweight structured-grounding pipeline rather than a full document-centric RAG system. Instead of retrieving arbitrary text passages from a large corpus, it injects structured scene memory, recent captions, and object-specific evidence into prompts. This design is more directly aligned with embodied reference resolution and with the low-latency demands of robot interaction.

\subsection{Multilingual Dialogue}
Multilingual interaction is a practical concern in social robotics because the deployment language, the robot's built-in speech capabilities, and the preferred language of external language models do not necessarily coincide. Pepper's native speech stack supports several predefined languages, but this does not remove the broader system-level challenge of keeping perception prompts, dialogue state, and robot output mutually consistent across languages \cite{softbank_alspeechrecognition_2026}. In practice, many modern systems resolve this mismatch through translation-mediated pipelines in which one language is used internally for prompt construction while user-facing input and output may be localized. This approach increases deployment flexibility, but it can also introduce semantic drift, especially for spatial relations, object names, and socially marked expressions. The multilingual layer in this thesis is therefore treated as an engineering feature built on top of grounded scene context rather than as an independent dialogue objective.

\section{Visual Perception for Social Robots}
Scene-aware dialogue requires more from perception than isolated frame-level labels. The robot must identify objects, preserve their identities across frames, maintain awareness of nearby people, and estimate relations that are useful for language generation, such as left-of, near, in-front-of, or occluding. In social robotics, these capabilities must also remain responsive enough to support turn-based interaction. A detector that is accurate but too slow, or a tracker that frequently changes identities, directly harms dialogue quality because the robot can no longer refer consistently to the scene.

These requirements motivate a perception stack that balances speed, configurability, and semantic richness. The implemented system therefore combines configurable detection backends, appearance-aware identity maintenance, Pepper-native person cues, and downstream scene graph generation. This combination reflects a broader robotics trend described in recent spatial perception literature: useful robot representations emerge when metric sensing, semantic labeling, and persistent structured state are connected rather than treated as separate subsystems \cite{mascaro_scene_2025, hughes_foundations_2024}.

\subsection{Object Detection}
Object detection remains the entry point for most scene-aware pipelines. One-stage convolutional detectors such as the YOLO family are widely used in robotics because of their favorable speed-accuracy trade-off, while transformer-based detectors increasingly narrow or surpass that trade-off by removing hand-crafted post-processing and improving end-to-end reasoning. RT-DETR is particularly relevant here because it shows that detector-transformer architectures can compete in real-time settings rather than remaining restricted to offline accuracy benchmarks \cite{zhao_detrs_2024}.

For a dialogue system, however, raw speed is not the only design axis. Closed-vocabulary detectors are efficient when the object categories are known in advance, but they limit the robot's ability to discuss rare or newly introduced objects. Open-vocabulary detectors address this limitation by accepting text-conditioned queries or broader label spaces. OWL-ViT is a canonical reference for open-vocabulary detection with vision transformers, while Grounding DINO further strengthens open-set grounding through language-conditioned detection \cite{minderer_simple_2022, liu_grounding_2024}. These models are particularly relevant to the thesis because they support a future-facing interpretation of scene-aware dialogue: the robot should not be limited to a single fixed object inventory when users can ask about arbitrary visible entities.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking and Re-Identification}
Tracking converts frame-wise detections into temporally persistent entities. For robot dialogue this is essential because conversation often refers not only to what is visible now, but also to what was visible a moment ago. If object identities are unstable, the robot cannot reliably answer follow-up questions such as ``what is next to the mug now?'' or ``is that the same person as before?'' Persistent tracking is therefore not merely a vision convenience; it is part of the referential substrate of the dialogue system.

SORT established a pragmatic tracking-by-detection baseline using Kalman filtering and Hungarian assignment, demonstrating that simple online association can be highly effective when detections are strong \cite{bewley_simple_2016}. Deep SORT extends this line by adding an appearance metric, making identity maintenance more robust under occlusion and re-entry \cite{wojke_simple_2017}. ByteTrack shows a later and stronger trend in modern MOT by associating both high-score and low-score detections to improve robustness \cite{zhang_bytetrack_2022}. The implementation in this thesis is conceptually closer to Deep SORT than to a full ByteTrack reproduction: it combines appearance embeddings and geometric consistency to maintain object identities and dormant tracks, because that design directly supports stable conversational references without requiring a heavier motion-centric stack.

\section{Semantic Scene Understanding}
Scene graphs provide a compact intermediate representation in which objects, attributes, and pairwise relations are made explicit. Rather than passing the raw image or an unstructured caption directly to the dialogue model, a scene graph exposes the parts of the scene that are most useful for grounded reasoning: which entities exist, which properties they have, and how they are related. Foundational work by Johnson et al.\ helped establish scene graphs as a structured semantic representation for visual reasoning and retrieval \cite{johnson_image_2015}. Later surveys show how this representation evolved into a major paradigm for scene understanding and downstream reasoning tasks \cite{zhu_scene_2022, agarwal_visual_2020}.

For robotics, the appeal of scene graphs is not only representational elegance. They provide an explicit bridge between perception and language. Attributes and relations can be inspected, validated, edited, and summarized; they can also be used to explain why the system responded in a certain way. This interpretability is especially important in a social robot that must discuss spatial relations with users while remaining configurable and debuggable by developers and operators.

\subsection{Scene Graph Generation}
Scene graph generation can be approached in several ways. Learned pipelines infer relationships directly from image features; rule-based pipelines derive relations from geometry, labels, or metadata; and recent multimodal approaches ask VLMs to produce relations from images, detections, or marked regions. The current literature increasingly treats these approaches as complementary rather than mutually exclusive \cite{zhu_scene_2022}. In the context of this thesis, that observation is important because no single method adequately covers the combined requirements of real-time performance, controllability, and semantic richness.

RelTR is a useful reference for learned relation prediction because it frames scene graph generation as direct set prediction with transformer-based subject-predicate-object decoding \cite{cong_reltr_2023}. At the same time, recent prompt-based work shows that large multimodal models can be steered toward more precise grounding when object regions are explicitly marked. Set-of-Mark prompting is especially relevant here because it transforms visual regions into explicit, referable symbols inside the prompt, which aligns closely with the thesis system's marked-object prompting path \cite{yang_set-of-mark_2023}. If mask-based or marked-region prompting is discussed in more detail, Segment Anything provides useful background on the broader segmentation infrastructure that enables promptable region-centric processing \cite{kirillov_segment_2023}. Together, these works motivate the hybrid design adopted in the thesis: fast rules for stable geometry, optional learned relation modules for structured prediction, and VLM prompting for semantically richer or harder-to-code relations.

\subsection{Neuro-Symbolic and Hybrid Reasoning}
Neuro-symbolic reasoning in this thesis refers to the combination of learned perceptual components with explicit structured state and symbolic relation logic. The key idea is not to replace learned models with handcrafted rules, but to place them inside an architecture in which intermediate state remains inspectable and actionable. This perspective is well aligned with robotics research on structured spatial representations. Dynamic scene graph work demonstrates how objects, humans, places, and higher-level topology can be combined into a representation that supports robot reasoning and action \cite{rosinol_3d_2020}. Hydra and subsequent work on the foundations of spatial perception argue even more strongly that hierarchical, persistent, multi-level representations are central to scalable robot understanding \cite{hughes_hydra_2022, hughes_foundations_2024, mascaro_scene_2025}.

This literature supports the design decision taken in the thesis. Purely end-to-end multimodal generation is attractive for coverage, but it weakens controllability and makes system behavior harder to inspect. Purely symbolic rules are transparent, but quickly become semantically shallow. A hybrid design allows the system to use geometric rules where they are reliable, VLMs where richer semantics are needed, and optional learned relation predictors where direct scene graph inference is beneficial. The resulting architecture is therefore neuro-symbolic in a pragmatic engineering sense: learned modules supply perception and semantic generalization, while symbolic structures preserve continuity, interpretability, and runtime configurability.

\section{Large Multimodal Models for Grounded Dialogue}
Large multimodal models extend language generation by conditioning on visual inputs, but their utility in robotics depends on more than benchmark accuracy. The central question is whether they can connect language to physically situated state in a way that remains useful for embodied systems. PaLM-E is one of the most influential references in this regard because it explicitly frames multimodal language modeling as embodied reasoning over sensor inputs and robot state \cite{driess_palm-e_2023}. Kosmos-2 extends this direction with stronger grounding to explicit visual entities, while InstructBLIP and LLaVA show how instruction tuning can make visual question answering and multimodal dialogue more general-purpose and accessible \cite{peng_kosmos-2_2023, dai_instructblip_2023, liu_visual_2023}.

These models are highly relevant to the thesis, but they are not adopted uncritically. Their strengths lie in flexible interpretation, open-ended explanation, and richer semantic judgment than handcrafted rules can typically provide. Their weaknesses include hallucination, sensitivity to prompt phrasing, and limited guarantees about structured consistency. For this reason, the system developed in this thesis uses multimodal models selectively: they support captioning, vision-grounded question answering, and relation enrichment, while the core dialogue context is still anchored in explicit scene memory. This arrangement preserves the expressive advantages of VLMs without surrendering the system's internal state representation to opaque end-to-end generation.

\section{Evaluation of Grounded Multimodal Systems}
Evaluating a grounded robot dialogue system requires analysis at multiple levels. Perception quality matters because missed detections and identity switches propagate into the dialogue layer. Scene graph quality matters because relation errors directly affect grounded language generation. Finally, end-to-end interaction quality matters because users experience the system as a whole rather than as isolated components. Existing HRI literature on LLM-enabled robots emphasizes that technical correctness and user perception cannot be collapsed into a single score \cite{kim_understanding_2024}. Likewise, Grassi et al.\ show that even when grounded conversation is feasible, latency and user experience remain central evaluation concerns in Pepper-based systems \cite{grassi_grounding_2024}.

From the scene graph perspective, the literature provides several useful metric families, including triplet-level correctness, relation-level correctness, and object-attribute evaluation \cite{zhu_scene_2022}. These metrics are especially relevant to the research pipeline implemented for this thesis, which evaluates strict triplets, binary relation structure, attribute quality, generation bias, and sensitivity to prompt context. At the system level, however, offline graph metrics are not sufficient on their own. A robot may obtain acceptable relation scores while still failing conversationally because of latency, mistracked entities, or poor reference selection. The evaluation strategy adopted in this thesis therefore combines component-level analysis with end-to-end grounded interaction assessment.
